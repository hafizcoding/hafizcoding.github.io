Cell culture · Protein assays

How cell density changes IL-6 signalingOngoing

I've been working in Dr. Pamela del Valle's lab since this past summer on a question that sounds simple: do crowded cancer cells make different things than spread-out ones? Specifically we're looking at interleukin-6 and its receptor in a mouse melanoma line (YUMM1.7). IL-6 signaling is tied to tumor growth and spread, and there isn't much published on how confluency alone affects it in these cells.

The reason I think this matters: in tissue engineering we design environments to mimic what happens in the body. If something as basic as how crowded a dish is changes what the cells produce, that changes how you read every model built on it. IL-6 is also a biomarker doctors track in cancer and inflammation, so it's not only an in vitro question.

We built the project from scratch. There was no shelf of ready-made reagents — we made our own buffers from recipes, including the lysis and running buffers for Westerns, which meant doing the calculations, weighing everything out, and checking pH before we could run anything. Slower than opening a kit, but I know what's in every tube and why.

It also didn't start the way we planned. The melanoma cells took weeks longer to arrive than expected, so we practiced on HEK 293T cells borrowed from another lab. That turned out to help — I got the basics down before they counted.

The thing I learned the most from was a bad standard curve. My first Bradford curve wasn't a straight line. Working out why taught me more than a clean one would have, and it's the same idea as calibrating any instrument: if the calibration is off, every number after it is off too. I spent similar time figuring out which reducing agent to use in our sample buffer for probing IL-6R, which showed me how many small decisions sit behind one experiment.

No results yet. We're running through the end of the fall semester, and the next step is comparing IL-6 secretion and IL-6R expression at 60% versus 100% confluency by ELISA and Western blot. I'll put the data here when we have it.

Embedded systems · Controls · Mechanical design

BLACKBIRD — beacon-homing delivery robot

I built this for the CIPASS summer engineering internship. It's a robot that drives toward a person instead of following a set path. You put your phone in BLE advertising mode with the name TARGET, and the robot scans for it, reads how strong the signal is, estimates how far away that puts you, and drives that way. If something gets within 15 cm an ultrasonic sensor stops it and it goes around. Once it gets close enough, a servo opens a hatch and drops whatever it's carrying.

I did the chassis in Onshape, wired and debugged the circuit, and wrote all the firmware.

I started on an Arduino Uno because that's what came in my kit. The problem is the Uno has no radio at all, so there was no way to do the phone part. I ended up switching the whole thing to an ESP32, which has Bluetooth built in. That took longer than the actual project. I kept getting upload and boot-mode errors, my USB-C adapter kept dropping out mid-upload, and I eventually killed one of the boards.

The two things that actually taught me something were both power problems. Running four motors and the ESP32 off one 9 V battery caused brownouts — the motors were slow, stuff got hot, and the board would reset partway through a run. I fixed it by giving the motor driver its own battery and powering the ESP32 separately. The other one was my fault: I wired the ultrasonic sensor's VCC to the 9 V rail and shorted it. It runs on 3.3 V. I replaced the sensor and moved the wire.

Biosignal processing

ECG heart-rate analyzer

A Python script that pulls real ECG recordings from the PhysioNet MIT-BIH database and calculates heart rate from them. I used record 100, which is sampled at 360 Hz. Real recordings are messy — there's baseline drift from the person breathing, 60 Hz hum, and noise from movement. You can see all of that in the top plot.

First I run a 4th-order Butterworth bandpass from 0.5 to 50 Hz. I used filtfilt instead of a regular filter because it runs the signal forward and then backward, which cancels out the phase shift. That matters here specifically: a normal filter shifts where the peaks land, and the whole measurement depends on peak timing.

Then I find the R-peaks by amplitude, with a minimum spacing of 100 samples between them. At 360 Hz that's 278 ms, which stops one beat from getting counted twice. It also caps what the script can detect at around 216 bpm, but no real heart rate goes that high so it doesn't matter. Averaging the gaps between peaks across the whole recording gives 75.5 bpm. The plot only shows the first 10 seconds but the number uses all of it.

Mechanical design · FEA

Bone plate — four-point bending FEA

My other projects are electronics and code, so I wanted one that shows I can do mechanical analysis. I modeled a straight 6-hole bone fixation plate in Fusion and simulated the standard bench test for bone plates: four-point bending, set up after ASTM F382. My prediction going in was that the highest stress wouldn't be in the solid middle of the plate but at the screw holes nearest the middle, because a hole concentrates stress around its edges.

The plate is 100 × 12 × 3.5 mm Ti-6Al-4V, the titanium alloy most plates are made from, with six Ø4.5 mm holes 16 mm apart. I put a 0.5 mm fillet on every hole edge, because a perfectly sharp edge makes FEA report a stress spike that isn't real.

The test setup needed a fix before it could run. I'd planned an 80 mm support span, which turned out to put the supports at ±40 mm — exactly on the outermost holes. The plate would have been resting on air. I moved the supports to ±32 mm and the load points to ±16 mm so every contact line falls in a gap between holes. Fusion can only apply a load to an edge that exists, so I made those lines by splitting the plate's faces with offset construction planes. One support is pinned in all three directions and the other only vertically, like a roller, so the plate can slide slightly as it bends. Fixing both ends would make it look stiffer than it is.

The prediction held. Peak von Mises stress was 143.7 MPa, right at the edges of the two innermost holes, a factor of safety of 6.1 against yield. The middle sags 0.19 mm and the ends tip up 0.17 mm past the supports.

I didn't want to just trust the colors, so I checked it by hand. Between the load points the bending moment is constant at 1600 N·mm. Through the narrowest section at a hole that works out to 104 MPa, so the hole is adding a concentration factor of about 1.4. Beam theory for a solid bar the same size predicts 0.15 mm of sag; the FEA gives 0.19 mm, which makes sense because the holes remove material and make the plate more flexible.

Next I'm rerunning it at 3.0 and 4.0 mm thick to see how stress and stiffness trade off, plus a mesh convergence check at the holes.
